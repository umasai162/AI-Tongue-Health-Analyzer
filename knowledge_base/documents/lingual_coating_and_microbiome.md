# Clinical Guide: Lingual Coating, Oral Microbiome, and Hydration Status
**Source:** International Journal of Oral Science & American Academy of Oral Medicine
**Scope:** Clinical Guide on Tongue Dorsal Biofilm and Furring

## 1. Composition of Tongue Coating
The dorsal lingual coating (often colloquially termed "tongue fur") consists of:
- Desquamated epithelial cells from filiform papillae
- Salivary proteins, mucins, and food debris
- Commensal oral microbial biofilm (principally Streptococcus, Veillonella, Actinomyces)

## 2. Coating Variations and Clinical Associations
- **Thin White Coating:** Physiological norm in healthy individuals with regular salivary clearance, mechanical mastication, and routine oral hygiene.
- **Thick White Coating:** Indicates accumulation of unsloughed keratin and microbial accumulation. Frequently linked to:
  - Dehydration / reduced salivary flow rate (hyposalivation / xerostomia)
  - Soft-food diet lacking abrasive mechanical shear
  - Mouth-breathing (nocturnal open-mouth sleep)
  - Inadequate tongue brushing or dorsum mechanical cleaning
  - Pseudomembranous Candidiasis (oral thrush): distinguishable if the white plaques can be scraped away leaving an erythematous bleeding base.
- **Yellow Coating:** Commonly caused by chromogenic oral bacteria interacting with food pigments, tobacco use, poor oral clearance, gastric reflux, or mild jaundice (hyperbilirubinemia if accompanied by scleral icterus).
- **Atrophic / Absent Coating:** Erythematous, glassy, depapillated dorsal surface ("bald tongue"). Seen in Hunter's glossitis, severe iron deficiency, pellagra (niacin deficiency), or Sjögren's syndrome.
