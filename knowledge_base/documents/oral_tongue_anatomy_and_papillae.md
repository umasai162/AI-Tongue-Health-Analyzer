# Clinical Guide: Lingual Anatomy, Papillae, and Dorsal Characteristics
**Source:** British Dental Journal & Journal of Oral Pathology & Medicine
**Scope:** Educational Reference for Visual Lingual Assessment

## 1. Normal Lingual Surface Anatomy
The dorsal surface of the human tongue is covered by specialized stratified squamous epithelium characterized by four varieties of lingual papillae:
- **Filiform Papillae:** Most abundant, slender conical projections providing mechanical friction. They lack taste buds and are keratinized. Hyperkeratosis or retention of desquamated filiform cells accounts for the normal physiological thin white coating.
- **Fungiform Papillae:** Mushroom-shaped, scattered predominantly along the tip and lateral margins. They appear as small red spots due to rich vascularity beneath thin non-keratinized epithelium. Prominent fungiform papillae are often mistaken by patients for lesions or inflammation ("strawberry tongue").
- **Circumvallate (Vallate) Papillae:** Arranged in a V-shaped line anterior to the sulcus terminalis at the posterior tongue base.
- **Foliate Papillae:** Parallel mucosal folds on the bilateral posterior-lateral margins.

## 2. Tongue Color Variations
- **Healthy Normal:** Light pinkish-red, moist, and pliable.
- **Pale:** Associated with lower mucosal perfusion, generalized anemia, or reduced systemic red blood cell mass. Requires clinical hematology verification (CBC/Ferritin) before concluding deficiency.
- **Erythematous (Red):** Increased vascularity, glossitis, vitamin B12 or folate deficiency, candidiasis, or post-hot beverage irritation.
- **Purplish/Cyanotic:** Localized venous stasis or central cardiopulmonary hypoxia. Must be clinically correlated with pulse oximetry.
